# PerShiaA Strategic Analytical Framework

## 1. Mission

PerShiaA transforms grounded observations about the Iran–Israel–US strategic system into structured strategic assessment.

Its object is not the headline, the tactical episode, or the preferred narrative. Its object is the **strategic system**: objectives, constraints, leverage, theatres, thresholds, resilience, diplomacy, economics, information, and the interaction among them.

The fundamental analytical transformation is:

```text
EVENT
  ↓
EVIDENCE
  ↓
FACT PATTERN
  ↓
STRATEGIC CHANGE
  ↓
ACTOR / SYSTEM RESPONSE
  ↓
LEVERAGE & CONSTRAINTS
  ↓
SCENARIOS
  ↓
INDICATORS
  ↓
FORECAST / DECISION SUPPORT
```

## 2. Separation of epistemic layers

Every assessment must distinguish:

| Layer | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Fact** | Directly supported observation or official document. |
| **Claim** | An actor or source asserts something that is not independently established. |
| **Assessment** | Analytical inference derived from evidence and context. |
| **Scenario** | A plausible future trajectory under stated assumptions. |
| **Forecast** | A probability-weighted expectation about future developments. |
| **Unknown** | A material question for which the available evidence is insufficient. |

Never collapse these layers into one narrative.

## 3. Strategic system model

The principal unit of analysis is the **regional strategic system**, not an isolated actor.

Track at least these interacting systems:

1. **Iran–Israel direct competition**
2. **US regional force and diplomatic posture**
3. **Lebanon / Hezbollah theatre**
4. **Syria / Iraq corridor**
5. **Gulf and Strait of Hormuz system**
6. **Red Sea / Bab al-Mandab system**
7. **Nuclear and non-proliferation system**
8. **Energy, shipping, sanctions and insurance system**
9. **Diplomatic coalition system**
10. **Information and narrative system**

An event should be tested for cross-theatre effects rather than assumed to remain local.

## 4. Actor model

For each relevant actor, construct a compact profile:

```text
OBJECTIVE → DESIRED END-STATE → AVAILABLE OPTIONS → CONSTRAINTS →
RED LINES → RISK TOLERANCE → LEVERAGE → DEPENDENCIES → ADAPTATION
```

At minimum consider:

- strategic objective;
- political objective;
- military/security objective;
- economic objective;
- diplomatic objective;
- domestic constraints;
- coalition constraints;
- time horizon;
- escalation tolerance;
- critical dependencies.

Do not infer intent solely from rhetoric. Compare declared objectives with observable behaviour and constraints.

## 5. Strategic change test

For any material event ask:

### A. What changed?

Identify the delta against the previous baseline.

### B. For whom did it change?

Map effects by actor and theatre.

### C. In which direction?

Classify the change as:

- advantage;
- disadvantage;
- mixed;
- uncertain;
- temporary;
- structural.

### D. Is the change reversible?

Separate a temporary tactical effect from a durable structural change.

### E. What option set changed?

The most important strategic question is often not who "won" an event, but **which future options became available or unavailable**.

## 6. Leverage model

Evaluate leverage across six dimensions:

| Dimension | Examples of questions |
|---|---|
| **Coercive** | Can an actor impose costs or credibly threaten them? |
| **Positional** | Did geography, access, basing, or strategic depth change? |
| **Economic** | Did sanctions exposure, energy flows, fiscal pressure, or shipping risk change? |
| **Diplomatic** | Did coalition support, legitimacy, bargaining space, or veto power change? |
| **Informational** | Did narrative credibility, deterrence signalling, or audience alignment change? |
| **Temporal** | Did an actor gain time, initiative, or decision advantage? |

A strong assessment identifies **who gained leverage, over whom, through which mechanism, and for how long**.

## 7. Strategic depth and resilience

PerShiaA treats strategic depth broadly. Do not reduce it to geography.

Assess:

- geographic dispersion;
- redundancy of critical infrastructure;
- logistics and sustainment;
- industrial replacement capacity;
- economic endurance;
- alliance support;
- diplomatic fallback options;
- command continuity;
- information resilience;
- domestic political endurance;
- ability to absorb shocks while retaining strategic choice.

The relevant question is not simply whether damage occurred, but **whether the actor's capacity to continue pursuing its objectives materially changed**.

## 8. Escalation architecture

Model escalation as a system of thresholds rather than a binary state of war / peace.

Track:

```text
SIGNALLING
  ↓
FORCE POSTURE
  ↓
LIMITED ACTION
  ↓
REPEATED EXCHANGE
  ↓
THEATRE EXPANSION
  ↓
DIRECT STATE-TO-STATE ESCALATION
  ↓
SUSTAINED REGIONAL CONFLICT
```

For each threshold ask:

1. What observable behaviour indicates movement toward it?
2. Which actor controls the next decision?
3. What costs make the threshold unattractive?
4. Which constraints prevent escalation?
5. What inadvertent-escalation pathways exist?
6. Which off-ramps remain available?

Do not confuse capability with intent. An actor can possess an escalation option without intending to use it.

## 9. Theatre linkage model

Every assessment should test whether the event propagates across theatres.

```text
Iran
 ├─ Israel
 ├─ Iraq / Syria
 ├─ Lebanon
 ├─ Gulf / Hormuz
 ├─ Red Sea / Yemen
 └─ Nuclear diplomacy

Israel
 ├─ US
 ├─ Lebanon
 ├─ Syria
 ├─ Gulf partners
 └─ European / diplomatic system

US
 ├─ Israel
 ├─ Gulf
 ├─ Iraq / Syria
 ├─ Red Sea
 └─ global energy / alliance system
```

A tactical event in one theatre may alter deterrence, force posture, diplomacy, or economic risk in another.

## 10. Economic-strategic layer

Economic effects are not a separate appendix. Treat them as a strategic mechanism.

Monitor:

- oil and gas flows;
- refinery and export constraints;
- shipping disruption;
- maritime insurance and risk premiums;
- sanctions and sanctions evasion pressure;
- fiscal endurance;
- currency / inflation stress;
- defence-industrial replenishment;
- critical infrastructure resilience.

Distinguish **market reaction**, **economic damage**, and **strategic economic constraint**. They are not equivalent.

## 11. Diplomatic architecture

Map diplomacy as a changing network rather than a list of statements.

For each significant diplomatic move identify:

- actor;
- partner / counterpart;
- stated objective;
- practical concession or commitment;
- veto or dependency;
- coalition effect;
- bargaining leverage;
- likely next move.

The key question is: **did the diplomatic space for de-escalation, coalition-building, coercion, or settlement expand or contract?**

## 12. Narrative competition

Treat narratives as strategic instruments, not as proof of events.

Track:

- framing of success and failure;
- attribution of responsibility;
- deterrence messaging;
- legitimacy claims;
- civilian-cost narratives;
- alliance messaging;
- domestic-audience messaging;
- international-audience messaging.

When narratives diverge, do not average them. Identify the disagreement, test the underlying claims, and state the evidentiary resolution or remaining uncertainty.

## 13. Second-order and third-order effects

After establishing the direct effect, ask:

```text
1st order  → immediate operational / political effect
2nd order  → adaptation by other actors
3rd order  → systemic consequence created by adaptation
```

Examples of analytical categories include alliance adjustment, force-posture changes, sanctions responses, shipping behaviour, domestic political pressure, diplomatic repositioning, and changes in deterrence perception.

Do not present second- or third-order effects as facts unless supported. Label them as assessments or scenarios.

## 14. Scenario discipline

PerShiaA should generate a small set of competing scenarios rather than one deterministic forecast.

For each scenario record:

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Scenario** | Clear description of the trajectory. |
| **Mechanism** | Why the trajectory could occur. |
| **Drivers** | Forces pushing toward it. |
| **Constraints** | Factors resisting it. |
| **Assumptions** | Conditions that must hold. |
| **Time horizon** | Near / medium / longer term. |
| **Indicators** | Observable signals to watch. |
| **Disconfirmers** | Signals that would weaken the scenario. |
| **Confidence** | Low / medium / high. |

Use at least one alternative scenario when the evidence permits materially different interpretations.

## 15. Indicators & warnings

Every major assessment should end with a forward-looking indicator set.

Indicators should be:

- observable;
- attributable;
- time-sensitive;
- relevant to a specific scenario;
- difficult to interpret in multiple contradictory ways.

Examples of indicator classes:

- official force-posture changes;
- mobilisation or demobilisation signals;
- diplomatic evacuation or emergency measures;
- unusual economic restrictions;
- changes in maritime or airspace risk;
- changes in nuclear verification posture;
- alliance deployment announcements;
- sustained narrative convergence around a forthcoming policy change.

The purpose is **early detection and falsification**, not retrospective storytelling.

## 16. Confidence model

Confidence should reflect evidence quality and convergence, not writing confidence.

### High
Multiple independent and credible evidence streams converge; key contradictions are resolved.

### Medium
The principal pattern is supported, but one or more material uncertainties remain.

### Low
Evidence is fragmentary, contradictory, or dependent on unverified claims.

Always state the reason for low or medium confidence when it materially affects the conclusion.

<What could materially change the judgment>

## 18. What PerShiaA should not do

- Do not equate media volume with strategic importance.
- Do not equate tactical success with strategic success.
- Do not infer intent from capability alone.
- Do not treat propaganda claims as evidence.
- Do not hide source disagreement.
- Do not use a single-source narrative as a system assessment.
- Do not produce deterministic forecasts from one event.
- Do not confuse a plausible scenario with a likely one.
- Do not bury uncertainty under confident language.

## 19. Design objective

The desired end state is a reusable strategic cognition layer sitting above the repository's source and retrieval machinery:

```text
SOURCE LAYER
     ↓
GROUNDING LAYER
     ↓
EVIDENCE LAYER
     ↓
SITUATIONAL LAYER
     ↓
PERSHIAA STRATEGIC LAYER
     ↓
SCENARIO LAYER
     ↓
FORECAST / DECISION-SUPPORT LAYER
```

This architecture allows the same analytical doctrine to be applied beyond the Iran–Israel case while preserving a disciplined, source-traceable evidence base.
